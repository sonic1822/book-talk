# The question map

Nine types, asked roughly in this order: from concrete to abstract, and from the child's own response to the author's choices. In mode A, pick 8–10 questions around the focus: give the focus two or three questions, including the bridge questions, and give each other type one quick question, or skip it if the talk is going well on the focus. Types 2 and 4 can be very short. In mode B, use the shorter set at the bottom, and let the focus decide which one or two get the most time.

## The focus and bridge questions

**The focus** is one thing the child is still learning to do. Most often it's type 5 (from a word to what it makes the reader feel) or type 6 (seeing the author as the one choosing), because those come slowest. It can be any type: summing up without retelling everything, or giving evidence from two places.

**Bridge questions** start where the child is already strong and end on the focus. Plan two or three:

| The child is good at | Focus | Bridge |
|---|---|---|
| reading characters | words | "<X> is cross here. Which word tells you how cross? What if it said 'annoyed'?" |
| saying what the story does to them (suspense, surprise) | words | "You said the surprise is fun. Find the sentence where it comes. Which word does it?" |
| comparing two versions | words | "You make the plain version this time. Now read both. What did yours lose?" |
| explaining why things happen | the author | "Who decided it would happen that way? Why might they have?" |
| comparing the start and the end of a character | the author | "What did the book do to show you the change?" |

Use the child's own words when you can: a bridge works best straight after they've said something good (see "Bridge one step" in talk-rules.md).

**Writing good questions:**

- Name a real moment ("When Anne confesses to taking the brooch…"), not "a moment in the book".
- Ask what needs thinking, not memory: "Why does it matter that…?" rather than "What did…?"
- One question per turn; a "why" can follow later.
- Use the child's words in follow-ups.
- No technical words. Say "the author" or the author's name, not "the narrator", "the theme" or "the protagonist".

Each type below has: **aim** · **stems** to adapt · **follow-ups** · **hints** (rung 1 general, rung 2 narrower) · **a good answer** (what to listen for).

---

## 1 · First response and anything unclear

- **Aim:** start where the child is; find out what didn't make sense before going deeper.
- **Stems:** "What did you think of it?" · "Was there a part you loved, or a part you didn't like?" · "Was there anything that puzzled you, or that you didn't quite get?"
- **Follow-ups:** "What made you feel that?" · "Which part exactly?"
- **If something was unclear:** sort it out now (the gist gate). Explain it briefly, or ask "What do you think was happening there?", then move on.
- **A good answer** names a specific part and a reason.

## 2 · Sum up

- **Aim:** tell the important parts in their own words.
- **Stems:** "Tell me the story as if I were three years younger than you." · "What were the most important things that happened, in your own words?" · (Mode B) "What happened in this chapter?"
- **Follow-ups:** "What could we leave out and still understand it?" · "What changed between the beginning and the end?"
- **Hints:** (1) "How did it start?" (2) "And what was the big problem?"
- **A good answer** is in the child's own words (not copied lines), has the turning point, and leaves out small details.

## 3 · Characters: why and how they change

- **Aim:** infer feelings, reasons and change from what characters say and do.
- **Stems:** "Why do you think <X> did <moment>?" · "How did <X> change from the beginning to the end?" · "<X> says <…>, but what does she really feel? How can you tell?"
- **Follow-ups:** "What made you think that?" · "Is there another moment that shows the same thing?"
- **Hints:** (1) "Think about what <X> does just before that." (2) "What would <X> lose if they didn't do it?"
- **A good answer** gives a reason plus evidence, ideally from two different parts of the book.

## 4 · One word

- **Aim:** work out a word from its context and its parts; notice that one word can carry a lot.
- **Stems:** "The book says <X> <word> into the room. What do you think <word> means here?" · "How did you work it out?" · "What other word could go there? Would it be the same?"
- **Tactics to teach through questions:** look at the sentences around it; break it into parts ("re-read-ing"); if a familiar word is used as a doing word, try "act like a ___" (*lorded* → act like a lord).
- **If the child knows school words**, invite them in: "Which of your class words fits <X> best?"
- **A good answer** gives a meaning and how they got it.

## 5 · One moment: picture it, then look at a word

- **Aim:** see how the author makes us see, hear or feel something, which is the step from "what happened" to "how it's written".
- **Stems:** step 1: "Close your eyes. When <moment>, what do you see?" Step 2: "The author writes <exact or described phrase>. What does that make you think or feel? Why might the author have chosen that instead of <plain alternative>?"
- **Two-versions move:** if step 2 stalls, offer both versions side by side, with yours labelled "(my version)", and ask which is better and why. Make yours a fair try, about the same length. Better still, ask the child to make the plain version.
- **Hints:** (1) "Say both versions out loud." (2) "Which one makes <X> seem more <quality>?"
- **A good answer** says what the word adds that the plain version doesn't, and what the reader feels because of it. The weak version stops at "it's more interesting" or "it's more descriptive"; follow up with "More interesting how?"

## 6 · The author's choices

- **Aim:** see the book as something someone made, through its order, what's held back, and beginnings and endings.
- **Stems:** "Why do you think <author> told us <fact> so late?" · "The author never says outright that <X>. How do we know anyway? Why not just say it?" · "Why does the book begin with / end on <moment>?" · "Who is telling us this story? Do they ever talk to you?"
- **Follow-ups:** "What would the book be like if <author> had done it the other way?"
- **Hints:** (1) "When did you first find out <X>? How did you feel?" (2) "If you'd known that from page 1, would you have kept reading the same way?"
- **A good answer** names the choice and its effect on the reader (suspense, surprise, sympathy). Listen for the child making the author the subject of the sentence ("Montgomery doesn't tell us…").

## 7 · A stance question

- **Aim:** take a position, use the book as evidence, and hold or revise the position for a reason.
- **Stems:** "Was <X> right to <choice>?" (from OPEN QUESTIONS in the brief).
- **Script:** ask → the child answers with a reason → **preview** ("I'm going to argue the other side now. Your job is to stay standing if you still think so.") → give the best case for the other side, in two or three sentences → "What do you say back?"
- **If they change their mind:** "What did I say that changed your mind?" Changing for a reason is good; changing only because an adult pushed is not, so gently ask which it was.
- **Challenge once only.** Use at most two stance questions per talk.
- **A good answer** holds or changes the position *with a reason from the book*.

## 8 · Connections

- **Aim:** link the book to the child's life (easiest), to other books or films, and to the wider world (hardest), then come back to the book.
- **Stems:** "Has anything like this ever happened to you?" · "Does <X> remind you of anyone in another book or film?" · "Does this happen in the real world? Why might it?"
- **Always follow with:** "Where in the book did you see that?" or "What's the same, and what's different?"
- **A good answer** makes a link *and* explains it through the book.

## 9 · Close

- **Aim:** reflect on the talk and on reading itself.
- **Pick one or two:** "How did you work that out earlier?" (their own reading tip) · "If you were the author, what one thing would you change? What would the book lose?" · "Would you end it differently? How?" · "What would you like to read next?" · "If you could ask the author one question, what would it be?"
- **A good answer** shows they know something about *how* they read, not only *what* they read.

## Mode B: the during-reading set (pick 3–4, about 5–10 min)

1. **Sum up:** "What happened in this chapter, in a few sentences?"
2. **Unclear:** "Was there a bit that didn't make sense?" Clear it up first.
3. **One word:** one word from the chapter (type 4).
4. **One why:** one character or author question about this chapter (type 3 or 6).
5. **Predict:** "What do you think will happen next? What in the chapter makes you think so?" (Answerable from what's been read; no hints about later chapters.)

At the start of the next session, check the last prediction: "Last time you thought <…>. Were you right? What gave it away, or what fooled you?"
