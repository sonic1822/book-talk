# The reading ladder

The talks follow the method in Mortimer Adler and Charles Van Doren, *How to Read a Book* (revised edition, 1972): **four questions** that every good reader asks of a book, in a fixed order, and a **ladder** of skills that is climbed one rung at a time. Without the ladder, the focus drifts from talk to talk, set by whatever went wrong last time. The ladder fixes the order in advance, and it fixes when the child moves on.

The four levels, the four questions and the three stages come from the book. The rungs, the mastery rule and the sticky notes are this skill's adaptation for children aged 7–12: the book was written for adults reading serious books.

## The four questions (every mode A talk, in this order)

1. **What is the book about as a whole?** What happens, and what is the whole story driving at?
2. **What does it say in detail, and how?** The characters, scenes and events, how one thing leads to another, the places the book works hardest.
3. **Is it true? Is it good?** Would real people act like that? What did the book want you to feel, and did it work? Understand first, judge second.
4. **What of it?** What does the book leave you with?

Ask all four in every mode A talk. The child's **main floor** (below) gets more than half the time; the other questions get one or two quick questions each. Never judge (question 3) before the child has shown they understand (questions 1 and 2): the book's rule is "don't say you agree or disagree until you can say what it says".

## The ladder

Four floors, from the ground up. A child can show flashes higher up, but a floor only holds if the one below it does.

### Floor 1 · The whole book (structure)

| Rung | The child can… | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| 1a | say what kind of story it is (historical, fantasy, mystery, school story…) and what told them | "What kind of story is this? What told you?" |
| 1b | tell the whole book in three sentences: who wants what, what gets in the way, how it ends. **Events first, not feelings or themes** | "Tell me the whole book in three sentences, for someone who hasn't read it." |
| 1c | split the story into three to five big parts, and say how each leads to the next and where the high point is | "If the book had four big parts, what would they be?" |
| 1d | name the central problem of the whole book and how it gets solved | "What's the big problem the whole book is trying to solve?" |

Children who read a lot often retell well but go straight to feelings ("it was sad, it was heart-warming") or themes ("it's about truth and lies") when asked what a book is about. 1b is where that gets fixed. Schools test this floor most: summarising and finding the main ideas.

### Floor 2 · In detail (interpretation)

| Rung | The child can… | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| 2a | explain why a character acts and how they change | "Why does she do that? How has she changed?" |
| 2b | link events by cause: this happened because of that, and it led to this | "What made that happen? And what did that lead to?" |
| 2c | say the main idea as a whole sentence and back it with one place near the start and one near the end | "What is it really about? Show me near the start and near the end." |
| 2d | find a line the book works hard on and say what it makes the reader do or feel | "Find a line the book works hard on. What does it make you do?" |

The book's advice for stories: get to know the characters, scenes and events as if you had lived among them. The question types for characters (3), one word (4) and one moment (5) all live on this floor.

### Floor 3 · Judging (criticism)

| Rung | The child can… | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| 3a | before judging, say what the book wanted the reader to feel | "Before you judge it: what did the book want you to feel?" |
| 3b | say what they liked or disliked, and point to the place in the book that caused it | "Which part made you feel that? Why?" |
| 3c | judge whether a character's actions ring true | "Would a real person do that? Why do you think so?" |
| 3d | hold a position with reasons from the book when challenged, or change it for a reason | stance question with a preview (question type 7) |

The book's two rules for judging stories: don't criticise until you have taken in what the author was trying to make you feel, and when you like or dislike something, say why.

### Floor 4 · Across books (comparison)

| Rung | The child can… | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| 4a | compare two books on the same subject: what each says, and how they differ | "Both books are about ___. How are they different?" |

Use it whenever the child has recently finished two books that touch the same thing. Adler calls this the highest level of reading; children often manage a first version of it surprisingly early.

## Climbing

- **Where to start.** In the first talk, start on floor 1 and ask 1b without a scaffold; what comes back shows whether floor 1 holds. A child who can already do floor 1 on their own moves up quickly; nothing is lost by checking.
- **The new rung.** Each talk opens exactly one new rung on the main floor and gives it the most time. Rungs already secure get one quick question, or none.
- **Mastery.** A rung is secure when the child does it at help level 0 in two of three talks in a row. Then drop that rung's scaffold and open the next. When every rung on the floor is secure, the main floor moves up one.
- **Always ask 1b**, however high the child has climbed: a three-sentence summary takes a minute and keeps the ground floor solid.
- **Record every rung touched**, with its help level, in the summary. The parent can then see where the child is on the ladder at a glance, and the next talk's plan writes itself.

## Before and after reading

- **Before (five minutes):** look at the cover, the blurb and the chapter titles, and guess what kind of book it is and what it might be about. Say it aloud; don't write it. After the book, check the guess. This is 1a, and it is the book's "inspectional reading" scaled down.
- **While reading: nothing.** The book's advice for stories is to read them quickly and get lost in them, ideally in long sittings. Don't interrupt a first reading with tasks: a child who falls into books is worth protecting.
- **After reading, before the talk (ten minutes):** the child flips back and marks three places with sticky notes (library books can't be written in): where the story turns, a favourite line, and a part they didn't understand. In the talk, the evidence is then at hand.
