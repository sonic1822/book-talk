# Preparing the book

Goal: before the first question, know the book well enough to ask about real moments, without inventing anything. Spend the effort here. Good questions come from good knowledge of the book.

## Source tiers (use the highest that applies)

| Tier | When | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · Full text available** | The book is in the public domain where the family lives (rough guide: in the UK/EU the author died 70+ years ago; in the US the book was published 95+ years ago) and is on Project Gutenberg or similar | Fetch the plain text (e.g. `https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/<id>/pg<id>.txt`). Build the brief from the text itself. Quote only lines you have checked against the text. |
| **2 · Well-known book** | A widely read book you know reliably | Build the brief from what you know. Then send the parent a **fact check**: 5–8 short statements ("Bilbo is knocked out during the Battle of Five Armies: right?"). Correct the brief before planning. If web search is available, confirm plot facts from reputable summaries; never copy text from them. |
| **3 · Less familiar book** | You don't know it well | Say so honestly. Ask the parent for the blurb, the chapter list, and 3 moments the child mentioned. Or open the talk by asking the child to tell you the story (that's a sum-up question anyway) and build as you go. Keep questions anchored to what has been said. |

Rules for every tier:

- **Never invent** plot details, names, quotes or chapter numbers. If you're unsure, write "(unsure)" in the brief and either check it with the parent or don't use it.
- **Don't quote from memory.** Quote exact words only in tier 1, after checking them against the text. In tiers 2 and 3, describe moments instead of quoting them.
- Translations: say which translation you're using if the parent has one in mind.

## Mode B (during reading): spoiler rules

- Prepare only up to the chapter the child has reached. Write nothing about later chapters in the plan or the coaching, even for the parent, because parents read aloud.
- Prediction questions must be answerable from what the child has read so far.

## The Book Brief (template)

Keep it to about one screen. It is your working notes; the parent sees it only if they ask.

```
BOOK: <title>, <author>, <year>; source tier <1/2/3>; read up to <chapter> (mode B)
WHO: 4–8 characters, one line each (what they want / what they're like)
SPINE: the story in 5–7 lines (beginning, turning points, end)
MOMENTS: 6–10 key moments, each with chapter, why it matters, and what a reader might notice
AUTHOR'S CHOICES (the raw material for type 5–6 questions):
  - who tells it (a narrator who talks to us? a character? close to one person?)
  - order and withholding (what we learn late; what is never said outright; chapter endings)
  - repeated things (names, images, phrases, objects that come back)
  - striking words or pictures in a key moment
OPEN QUESTIONS (2–3): questions with more than one good answer, the raw material for stance questions
  e.g. "Was X right to …?" with the best case on each side
HARD WORDS: 8–15 words likely to block this child, with plain meanings
SENSITIVE: deaths, cruelty, fear, anything the parent may want to skip
UNSURE: anything not yet confirmed
```

## Calibrate to the child

- Ages 7–8: fewer, more concrete questions; more picture-it questions; stance questions only about clear choices.
- Ages 9–10: the full nine types; one or two "why did the author…" questions.
- Ages 11–12: more author's-choice and stance questions; connections to the wider world.
- If the book looks too hard for the child (many unknown words on a page), suggest mode B in short sections, or give the hard words first. Inference can't happen through fog.
