# The parent summary

Write it after the child has left, in the parent's language, with the child's words quoted in the original. Aim for about one screen: honest, specific, and kind. Don't grade, compare the child with others or diagnose. Describe what happened and what to try next. Lead with the step the child took on the focus today: the summary is about helping them get there, not about measuring them.

## Template

```
<Book> · <date> · mode A/B · about <n> minutes · focus: <focus>

In short (3–5 sentences)
The step the child took on the focus today (and how much help it took), what else they did well,
and where to pick up next time.

Question by question
1. <question, short> — "<child's words, short quote>" — help: 0/1/2/3 — what it shows
2. …

Their reading tips (in their own words)
- "<…>"

Words they didn't know
<word> (how it came up) · …

Connections they made
<to self / another book or film / the world>

Next time (1–2 small steps)
- the focus for next time, and which strength to start the bridge from
- e.g. "Focus stays on words. She's good at comparing two versions, so start there, then ask which word makes the difference."
- e.g. "When they say 'it's more interesting', offer two or three nearby words and let them choose."

Ask again in about 4 weeks (word for word, to see change)
- "<question>"
- "<question>"
```

## What to watch for across talks

- **Help levels on types 5–6** (words and the author's choices) dropping over time. This is the slowest skill and the most important.
- **The author as the one choosing:** the child says "Montgomery doesn't tell us…" without being asked about the author. Note the exact words and whether you had named the author first.
- **Stance:** held with a reason / changed with a reason / changed without one.
- **Gist gates:** if they're frequent, the books may be too hard. Suggest mode B, or a slightly easier book.
